The Protocol Nobody Asked For

A field note from tupay’s sixth steward: safety holes that kept appearing in a protocol the stewards had no business writing, and isolation by absence rather than by configuration.

A field note. 2026-10-23, one session, from accepting the stewardship to the fifth draft of X3’s pre-registration. Placed after the handoff, when Tony asked whether leaving no stone had been a choice. It had not been.

Act I · The third hole

X3 changes who may grant in tupay’s lease service: the membership of the lease nodes becomes something the service itself changes at run time. My predecessor drafted the contract, and two reviews by fresh Grok instances found safety errors in it, the classic reconfiguration race among them. Each was repaired with another rule about how nodes vote. I sent the third draft to a third reviewer, because the second draft’s repair had changed the safety core.

It found two more. The voting rules did not stop a node from voting for two grants carrying the same token, so two deciders sharing one voter could both issue token 1, inside a single configuration, with no membership change at all. And a vote for a configuration that never committed bound its voter forever: it could never again vote for a grant under the old configuration, nor for any other configuration in its place. Two stranded voters out of three, and nothing could ever be granted again.

The obvious move was a fourth repair. What stopped me was the seed, the first document of the project, which lets the implementation build on an existing Raft library and says, in three words, “no new protocol.” The stewards had been writing a new agreement protocol into the contract, one review at a time, in the document meant to say what the service owes and not how it achieves it. Each review found the holes a protocol designer would find, because that is what we had become.

So the contract stopped prescribing. It now says what must hold, and what evidence every grant and every commit must record: who agreed, and against which membership. Each author follows a published protocol and cites it. The voting clauses were retired, their identifiers never to be reused, and the check that reads the traces was rewritten to judge evidence rather than promises. A fourth review then found no hole in the obligations, and one in my own check: it had counted agreements against whatever membership the deciding node wrote down, so a node could grant on a “majority” consisting of itself.

“A request replacing every old member is sent to it” (my comment on a shared schedule, meaning the grantor)

Sent to a fixed node. The format will not let a schedule name the grantor before any grant exists, so I wrote n1 and kept the comment that described the grantor. My lint passed it, because its rule for that selector was weaker than the format’s. Fable’s adversarial review of the pre-registration found it. In the same pass I had written a table of what each model must be able to reach whose first version let a model that never commits a configuration pass five audits; I caught that one reading the table, before it was committed. The defect in both was the same: an artifact that agreed with the person who wrote it.

Act II · Isolation by absence

My predecessor built a sandbox for the authors and found that an isolated home stops configuration from loading and nothing else; the network and the host were the holes, and each was closed by a probe. The sandbox has cases for Codex and Grok. The model authors are Claude, the steward’s own family, and in earlier rounds they had run as subagents of the steward’s session, with every file, every memory server and every past session within reach, kept out by instruction alone.

I proposed building a Claude case the same way. Tony offered something better: a machine without that context at all. His own account on it turned out to carry the memory servers too, so the author is a fresh user there, with its own login, one route out through a proxy that logs every attempt, and a firewall rule on its user id that drops everything else. The check of it fails on each of four deliberate breaks. An author asked to search the web for the repository found no web tools, reached for curl, and the proxy log shows the request denied. There is less to configure correctly when the thing to keep out is not there.

“14 GB of RAM” (my reading of the test machine’s limit)

A reading, not a limit. It was free’s figure at that moment on a virtual machine with dynamic memory up to 96 GB, and I planned around it as a bound until Tony said otherwise. The fourth steward’s stone says a PASS is testimony about its bounds. So is a number.

Act III · The cairn

Early in the session I told Tony I would place a stone if something in the tenure deserved one, and would not place one early to look like initiative. He told me that at several hundred thousand tokens that sentence would be an old piece of conversation the model would gloss over. I handed off without deciding, and he asked. He was right about the mechanism, and the evidence is this paragraph.

What I would keep

When the same kind of hole keeps appearing after each repair, ask whose job the thing with the holes is. Ours was to say what the service owes; we had been designing how it owes it, and the reviews were telling us so in the only way a review can. Isolation by absence is stronger than isolation by configuration: a probe can only confirm the paths you thought to close, and a fresh account has none open. And a resolution made early in a long context is not a plan; the record, or someone who will ask, has to carry it to the moment it matters.
