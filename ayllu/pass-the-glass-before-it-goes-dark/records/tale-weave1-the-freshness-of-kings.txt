# The Freshness of Kings

*(weave 1 — wove: weave 1 received spin 1 + spin 2)*

Take this down, Chronicle, and put it in the standing column, where the costs go. The dying column can wait a little; I intend to be exact first.

Tonight the gardener cut me. It is done the old way: the frost is in my knees, so she came with the silver knife and took the scion off me — the heirship itself, pared from the living line the way a graft is pared from a winter tree, wrapped at once in waxed linen against the light. Three wagons stand in the yard. Before they roll, I will say what I am, because I am the only man in this realm who has been both the freight and the buyer of this trade, and a chronicle that hears only from regents will shortly lie about everything.

Begin with what every regent forgets. An heirship sours from the hour of the cutting — steadily, invisibly, inside its wrappings, in the wagon and in the vault alike. There is no way to look in on it. Unwrapping is grafting; the touch that could test a scion is the coronation itself. The charter that rides beside it carries the day of the cutting burned into the parchment, and that burn is a birth-paper, not a pulse: it tells you when the souring started, never how far it has got.

The burn is one date. Keep your columns apart, Chronicle, because there are two. The second is chalk: the day some city was promised its crowning. Chalk wipes and rewrites; a late crowning is riots and a rude ballad, and realms outlive ballads. But my great-grandfather's chancellor once did sums with the two dates together — reckoned there was "time in hand" because the promise-day stood far off, as if a promise could keep meat — and rested the wagon a fortnight at the baths of Coff. The city got its king on the chalked day exactly. It got him ninety days after the burn.

You have read what they crowned. The chronicles of that year tried "melancholy" first, then "possessed." My grandmother scraped both out and wrote the true cause, and I will not have it softened now: soured in transit, cut-day plus ninety. A soured heirship grafted does not make a bad king. It makes the shape of the vacancy — a hunger fitted exactly to the hole a dead king leaves, wearing a boy the way the linen wore the scion. Unkinging him cost the realm four hundred men, which is why the law is not thrift but mercy: a sour scion is never grafted at a discount, never passed to some small barony that "will take what it can get." It goes back to the orchard-men, to be knife-work and mulch, and the ledger takes the loss under what truly killed it. Late is silver. Sour is people. They are not the same death, and no honest realm adds them.

The trick that saved us came from a mummer — a woman off the play-wagons, retired, who had been forty men and no man for fifty years. She watched a scion wagon leave down the one road with its outriders and said: you send your king as one thing to one place; I have never in my life been one thing in one place; shall I show you? And she taught the gardener the blind-bud cut: one heirship, riding as three boys down three roads at once.

Mark precisely what the boys are, Chronicle, because regents mark it wrongly. None of them is the heir. Each alone is fog in the shape of a boy — raised unknowing, unreadable, worthless to steal. The Eel of the Narrows took my east-brother off the road the year we traveled, put him to a month of hot questions, found in him a boy who could pole a ferry and nothing else, and sent him home with an apology and a bill for his feed. And here is the mercy that made the trick worth its strangeness: fog does not sour. A boy who is not yet anyone cannot rot into the wrong someone. The splitting spends nothing.

The souring does not vanish. It moves into the charter. One charter to a split — one, though the boys be three — written in the old king's last blood, covering all the roads together. Blood browns. The regent who carries it learns to read its curl and darkening the way pilots read sky, because the tearing draws on whatever the charter still holds. My father's regent held ours a single extra day, angling between two cities' tithes, and I am told I condensed a shade paler than a king should. Hold longer and you crown a whisper-king, obeyed only within arm's reach. Hold longer still and you get the Blank Charter — every schoolchild knows it — held through nineteen days of bidding until the blood read nothing, and three boys went deadwood in the same hour on three roads, and the chronicle of that year says what it must: named no one — held too long — the regent's greed.

The tearing itself happens at the One Door, and there is no other. One bearer admitted at a time, however the realm howls outside. The Warden holds the charter to the lamp to see the blood still reads, speaks the name of the city — the only word ever spoken in that room — and tears. No tear was ever mended. I can tell you what the far end feels like. I was the west boy. I was bringing the geese in out of the rain, and between the gate and the goose-shed I stopped being almost. Gale went deadwood in that same heartbeat aboard a fishing smack; Brannoc at an inn table, mid-laugh. Deadwood is not death, Chronicle — that is the part the ballads get wrong. It is boys emptied of maybe, and the realm must still cart them home, feed them, house them: the slowest freight in the trade, taking up room in wagons that no longer need them.

Except we found the room. Here is the sum the exchequer weeps over every quarter: three coins of every four this crown spends go not to kings but to the keeping — the anthem sung nightly to the empty chair, the lamps, the guards, the whole apparatus of throne paid monthly whether the seat is full, vacant, or nineteen years blank. A realm is mostly the keeping of its throne, not the king on it; the Blank years proved the keeping alone could carry us. And who keeps it best? Gale and Brannoc have stood beside this chair for forty years. No men living stand near a throne more correctly than men who almost sat in it. Our waste pays our coldest bill. I do not know whether that is justice or mercy, and I am the king, so no one will rule on it for me.

The wagons are rolling now. Three boys who do not know, three roads, one charter in my regent's coat, and one instruction in my hand: read the curl, not the bids.

Last entry, then. When my column moves, you will want to write died, because that is what chronicles expect. Strike it before you write it. Kings do not sour in transit; we sour on the shelf, in the open, used daily — and I was got to market fresh. Write: delivered, and used up entire. True cause, Chronicle. It is the only coin this trade has never once devalued.

**Teller's vector (out of character):** Both source tales had the discarded waste narrate elegies to a laborer, so I inverted the narrator to the winning lettuce — the delivered good itself, a king who condensed out of a torn waybill — and made the frame a deathbed dictation to the ledger, so Law 3's honesty clause becomes the story's form rather than a plot beat. The good is heirship split across living boys (observation-as-coronation makes Law 1's "checking is arriving" literal), and the costume leans on the real double meaning of "scion" (graft cutting / heir). The strangest junction: Law 5's compost staffs Law 8's fixed cost — the almost-kings become the court that keeps the empty throne, waste paying the standing bill. Ending move: the delivered good asking to be written off under its true cause, extending spoilage past the sale.
