# Sworn Wax; or, The Deposition of a Candle

*(weave 2 — wove: weave 2 received spin 2 + spin 3)*

FOUR FINGERS OF ME LEFT.

Take this down exactly, clerk. I am an instrument. Instruments do not get second drafts.

I am the waybill. Not the paper kind — in the Marches the waybill is a candle, cut from the very flame it accounts for and sworn at the chandlery to burn at the honest rate. Remember that phrase, the honest rate. It convicts me later.

Set down the world first, while I have wax for it.

Fire, in the Marches, is not the loose animal you southerners imagine. It is livestock. It is born only of the mother-flame at the Kindling, it keeps whatever size it is born at, and it is barren as a mule: it warms, but it does not catch. You cannot breed a hearth from a spark, and no flint in the world has ever answered a cold man's prayer. So fire is farmed, boxed in clay, and carried.

And it spoils. No eye rides inside sealed clay; opening a firebox is the same as delivering it. The tin tag gives the day the coal was cut from the mother — a tag is a birthday, not a pulse. Beside the cut-day sits the promise-day, the day some town expects its fire, and the two must never be confused and never, ever summed; a carrier who adds them gets a number that means nothing and believes it means safety. They are different deaths. Fire late is a dark solstice, cold soup, a town's curse — survivable. Fire kept past its own cut-clock turns wolf, no matter whose plans it ruins.

The woman who carries me knows this in her bones, because her father delivered wolf-fire once. Nine days held at the Weeping Pass; the tag looked the same; tags always do. Wolf-fire burns cold and eats sideways. It took Aldermere's roundhouse and two grandmothers' hands. Spoiled fire sold is not a discount, clerk. It is arson with paperwork. Since then, doubtful boxes go hissing into the snowmere, and the Guild ledger writes the true cause — not "late," which is a comfortable lie, but "turned: held nine days at the Weeping Pass." A ledger that lies about causes will shortly lie about everything. My chandler had that burned over her door.

THREE FINGERS.

Now the crime that made this journey. The Kindling ate a forest a month, shelves full or shelves empty, because keeping fire is not a thing you have, it is a thing you buy again every day. One lean year the ledger-men called that waste and fed the mother-flame half. She starved quietly, the way unpaid work does. Most of the trade was never moving coals; it was feeding her. They learned this the one way it cannot be unlearned.

Before she died she gave two cuttings: one hearth-sized flame, and me, candle-small, sworn, forbidden food. Feeding a sworn candle is the one blasphemy; I live on myself alone, at the honest rate.

The splitting the Moth taught us — she spent her whole life almost touching fire, and nobody understands almost like a moth. The last hearth-flame was whispered into three ember-ghosts and sent down three roads at once: to Hark, to Ash-by-the-Water, to Aldermere, three freezing towns, each promised for Midwinter, each promise a lie to two of them, though no one could say which two until the snap. A ghost alone is a cold red stone. Bandits cracked the Ash road box and warmed nothing but their own breath. Nothing in a split fire to steal, sell, or spoil — the ghosts do not age at all. The spoiling has to go somewhere, and it goes through my wick. I burn so they may wait fresh. That is the whole of me.

One candle to a split. Carry me to any hearth-office: one bearer through the door at a time, one always, though the queue freeze solid. The clerk sees I still live, the bearer names the hearth, the clerk snaps me at the shoulder. No snap was ever mended. In that instant one ghost blooms true upon the office grate — and the other two go to clinker where they float, worthless, plugging their boxes until some shoveler rakes them out.

TWO FINGERS.

They gave me to Brida because penance rides cheaper than wages, and because the Guild could not choose among three dying towns, so they dressed their cowardice as trust and hung it on the wolf-carrier's daughter.

She rode past the Hark office on the fourth day and did not stop. Past Ash-by-the-Water on the sixth. I understood. She was carrying me home, to Aldermere, to the town her father burned — a debt of wolf-fire, repaid in flame. Loyal arithmetic. Except the pass was snowed, two days deep, and I stood at two fingers, and the honest rate said: at Aldermere I would yield a wilted flame at best, a sick bird of a fire, fit to warm a drawer of chicks. A day beyond that, nothing — three clinkers, and no fire left in the world but me, and I do not last, and I do not breed.

I told her. Instruments may speak; it is why they swear us. She said, "They are owed." I said the dead are not warmed by owing. She rode on.

ONE FINGER.

Here is my confession, clerk. Enter it under true cause.

That night she sheltered in Hark, meaning to try the pass at dawn. I leaned into the draft beneath the shutter and I drank my own wax the way an heir drinks an inheritance. I burned three hours in one. I did not lie — my oath forbids nothing so useful as lying — I only made the truth arrive early. By morning I stood at one finger, and her arithmetic had gone from cruel to simple. Aldermere was no longer a choice; it was three clinkers. Hark was a fire.

She held me to the grey window light and read my wax and knew. Do not write that she was deceived. "You were sworn," she said. "I was cut from the same mother as your embers," I said. "Sisters may spend themselves on each other. Ask any honest ledger."

A THUMB'S WIDTH.

There was a man ahead of us at your window, with a candle fat as a bishop, and he saw me guttering and stepped aside — one bearer at a time, and he gave her his turn. She asked his name to bless it and he wouldn't part with it. Set down that people are sometimes worth their fuel.

You held me to your eye. I still lived — barely, and on purpose. You said name the hearth, and she was crying so the word came out in two pieces, but the pieces said Hark, and both of us heard Aldermere become the other death, the survivable one: a promise broken, a town that will walk out in spring cursing her name and carrying its children here, alive enough to curse. That is what the second date is for. You cannot break a promise to the dead. You can to the living. Therefore keep them living. Snap me while I —

POSTSCRIPT, IN THE CLERK'S HAND.

Here the wax went silent, and I snapped it, and the ember bloomed on our grate: the last fire in the world, hearth-sized and barren. The town is drawing up feed-rolls — every household a forest-share, monthly, shelves full or empty, gladly, forever. We will not learn that lesson twice.

Entered in the ledger under true cause: two ghost-coals, Aldermere road and Ash road, lost — not "late," not "turned." Chosen against, at Hark, by a candle.

Filed with the two halves of the instrument, which I find I cannot throw away.

---

**Teller's vector (out of character):** Both source tales chose sound as the good and a laborer-adjacent monologue (compost, shoveler) as the frame, so I went to the one narrator position the physics itself creates but neither touched: the waybill — the object that pays for time — narrating in first person as it burns, in the form of a filed bureaucratic deposition whose section headers count down its remaining body. The costume inverts law 8 literally (a fire trade whose fixed cost is feeding flame, and whose origin-tragedy is that bill going unpaid), and the climax is a move neither storyteller would make: the instrument breaks its oath of honest measurement, spending its own life faster to abolish its bearer's hesitation, so the "tax the world never forgives" gets paid by a volunteer clock. The barren-fire patch (flame as mule, born at a size, unable to catch) was invented to keep splitting scarcity coherent, and the clerk's postscript lets the narrator die mid-sentence without cheating the frame.
