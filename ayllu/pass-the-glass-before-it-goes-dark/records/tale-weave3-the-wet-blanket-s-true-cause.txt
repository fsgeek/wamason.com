# The Wet Blanket's True Cause

*(weave 3 — wove: weave 3 received spin 3 + spin 1)*

To the Worshipful Company of Wet Blankets, assembled in Sobersward, from Brother Marn, Bore of the Second Watch: this notice of resignation.

Bylaw the ninth — which I have enforced upon better men than myself — requires a departing brother to write his cause under its true name, never its comfortable one. Very well. But a true name comes at the end of a road, Brothers, so you will ride the road first, as I did.

You know the trade we serve, though we stand at its cold end and are not thanked. A joke is cut from a survived moment — from the lightning that misses, the fever that breaks, the ice that cracks a breath after the child steps off it. Relief is the sap; the jestwrights tap it while it runs. And from the hour of its cutting a joke stales — secretly, steadily, sealed in its cedar box, and there is no way to ask a boxed joke whether it still lands. Telling is the only question, and telling is arrival. The papers pasted to the lid record a birth, not a health.

Two dates ride every lid, and the guild flogs apprentices who confuse them or — Fates forbid — add them together. In iron: the day the joke was cut, after which it stales on its own schedule and cares nothing for anyone's feast. In chalk: the day some far occasion expects it. Chalk wipes and rewrites; a feast kept waiting is a broken promise, an apology, a refund — survivable. Iron moves for nothing.

The jestress Pell learned the difference the way everyone learns it: on someone else's face. Years ago, running late to a wake — yes, we jest at wakes; nothing wants laughing at like death — she opened an eleven-day box rather than arrive with nothing. The joke came out stale, and a stale joke delivered is not a weak joke. It is a wound. It turned in the air and came down as mockery; the room laughed the wrong laugh, the kind with teeth, and the widow heard her grief made sport of and never fully unheard it. Since then Pell's stale stock goes unspoken into the Groanyard, tilled under with lime, and into her ledger under its true cause — never "late," which is the comfortable lie, but "staled: held four days at the flooded ford." Two kinds of death, two columns, no blending.

And here is where we come in, Brothers, we of the wet blanket. Jokes keep only in gravity. Merry air stales them double-quick; a town that giggles year-round can store nothing. So the jest-trade hires us to keep Sobersward glum — droning the tax rolls in the market, scheduling drizzle, standing about in grey. The town is the cellar and we are its cold, and cold is not a thing a town has; it is a thing we make fresh daily, in three watches, through festival and famine alike. Our bill goes out at every month's end whether the cellars under the guildhall hold three hundred boxed jokes or none, and the jestwrights' accountant curses us each quarter with the same arithmetic: three coins in four spent on men standing solemnly still, one thin coin on jokes actually carried. They pay. They always pay. Hold that in mind; it is the hinge of the ending.

The spring the river spared us, the finest joke cut in a century came off that morning's relief — the whole town alive at once, distilled. Iron date: flood-morn. Chalk: the Duke's daughter's wedding, twelve days east, and the flood had eaten half the bridges between. So Pell did the new trick, the one the retired conjurer taught her: she whispered the joke apart into three expectancies and sent them down three roads — river-road, hill-road, salt-road. Understand what those wagons carried. None of them carried the joke. Each alone was pure setup, a leaning-forward with no release; bandits pried open the river-road box and the chief held the fragment to his ear and heard only that someone, somewhere, was about to say something, and he kept it, and stood inclined slightly forward for a year, waiting. Nothing in a split joke can be told, sold, or staled — expectancy keeps forever. The staling has to go somewhere, and it goes into the timing-slip: one slip for all three wagons, on which is inked the pause before the landing, nine heartbeats long, losing a beat a day whether you ride hard or sleep late. Pell carried it herself. Tear it with the pause fat and the joke lands whole; tear it thin and the joke arrives wilted, a thing that must be explained; let the pause run out and there is nothing left to tear anywhere in the world.

On the sixth day the chalk moved. The groom's ship sat becalmed; the wedding would wait a sennight. Lateness — survivable. But count it, Brothers, as Pell counted it in an inn with the slip on the table: the pause would be dust three days before the new chalk. Hold the joke for its promised market and she would deliver a wound to a bride, and that she would not do twice in one life. The joke had to land soon, somewhere with ears, or die everywhere. And the only full room within a thin pause's ride, on the salt-road, on the ninth night of the month, was ours: the Dull Assembly, forty bores in session — the one audience in the world contractually forbidden to laugh.

I was Doorkeeper that night. One bearer at the wicket; that is the law and I have kept it against dukes. I admitted her alone, counted her pause against my own pulse — three heartbeats, but alive — and required her to name the hall. She named ours. I told her no one within was permitted to laugh. She said: "Permitted is chalk, old man. The ninth day is iron." And I tore the slip.

I will not write the joke here; ink is a box it does not travel in. I will write what it did. Three heartbeats from wilting, it landed whole in a room built against it, and I — thirty-one years on the cold, Brothers — I laughed. First and loudest. And in that same instant, on the hill-road, a wagon of expectancy turned to groans, and the drayman shoveled groans into the dark till dawn, for a groan takes exactly as long to unload as its laugh would have lasted, and this laugh was long. On the river-road the bandit chief heard his stolen fragment die in his ear at last, and straightened, and went home.

Pell's ledger says, in her own hand: "Torn early at Sobersward. Chalk moved; iron didn't." Not "wasted." The wedding got its refund and a bride unwounded, free to be teased fresh some other year.

And you, Brothers, will bill the town entire at month's end, for the gravity we kept and the night we didn't, because boredom is fed daily or not at all, and the cold is owed even when the shelves stand empty. Take my last wage out of it. I have been paid by other means.

Now the true name, as the bylaw asks. Not "laughed on duty" — that is only the chalk of it. Write: torn open at his one market, after long storage, with three heartbeats to spare. The tearing cannot be un-torn, Brothers.

Neither can I.

---

**Teller's vector (out of character):** Both source tales were elegies about death, last words, and grief, framed as a veteran addressing a newcomer — so I inverted the register entirely: a comedy about perishable jokes, where "stale joke," "delivery," "timing," and "landing" make the eight laws feel native rather than costumed. I moved the frame to an epistolary bureaucratic form (a resignation letter whose bylaw mirrors the ledger's true-cause rule), made the narrator an employee of Law 8 itself (the fixed-cost cold, which both tales treated as background pathos), and drove the plot with the one mechanism neither tale used — the movable chalk date, so that Laws 2, 3, and 6 jointly force a fresh masterpiece to be torn early into the one room forbidden to receive it. The ice-house laughing, and still sending its bill, is the ending both tales made possible and neither would have written.
